Topic 1 Ms G.H Adeoba

What is Electronic Security?

Definition, why it matters for e-finance, and the four pillars of a public-policy response.

Source
Speaking broadly, electronic security (e-security) is any tool, technique, or process used to protect a system's information assets. E-security enhances or adds value to an unprotected network, and is composed of soft and hard infrastructures.
Plain EnglishE-security is everything — policies and technology — that keeps a computer system and its data safe from people who shouldn't have access to it.
  • Soft infrastructure — policies, processes, protocols and guidelines that protect the system and data from compromise.
  • Hard infrastructure — the hardware and software needed to protect the system/data from threats, whether from inside or outside the organization.
  • Security should be proportional to the value of the activity it protects — it is fundamentally a risk-management and risk-mitigation tool, not an end in itself.
  • Services in this growing industry: active content filtering, firewalls, intrusion detection, penetration testing, cryptographic tools, authentication mechanisms.

Why E-Security Matters for Finance

As banking moves online (projected to approach 50% in industrial countries, rising from roughly 1% to 10% in emerging markets), trust and confidence become essential infrastructure. Because weak regulatory environments in one country can be exploited to attack institutions in another, the systemic risk of attacks increases as markets integrate — and emerging markets, with the least financial and institutional depth, are the most vulnerable.

The Problems of Economic Incentives

  • A small number of vendors supply interlinked services to many e-finance providers, raising competition-policy and conflict-of-interest questions.
  • Telecoms, energy and financial services are critical infrastructures — disruption causes significant economic damage, so there is a public-interest case for government regulation.
  • Market failure: inadequate incentives exist to report e-security breaches accurately — timely, accurate reporting with strong penalties is needed.
  • The Internet makes financial-service access global and borderless, so risk mitigation requires cooperation within and between countries.
  • Policy must balance competing concerns — e-security is one part of risk management, not an end in itself; tradeoffs exist between cost, bank size, and sophistication of security.

Topic 3 Ms G.H Adeoba

Decomposing the Risks Associated with Electronic Transactions

Money laundering, E-gold, and the six risk categories of e-finance.

Money Laundering & E-gold

Source
Former IMF Director Michel Camdessus estimated the global volume of money laundering at between two to five percent of global GDP, a range encompassing $600 billion to $1.8 trillion.

E-gold was an electronic currency, 100% backed by gold bullion, operating in units of weight rather than national currency — used as an example of how a "non-financial institution" can become a de facto money remitter with no real records, few diligence standards, and no suspicious-activity reporting, based in jurisdictions (e.g. Luxembourg) with strong secrecy laws.

The Six Risk Categories

RiskWhat it means (as supplied)
Systemic RiskIntrusion/interruption in a payment system's electronic messaging can create system-wide exposure; concentration of hosting services in one provider is a single point of failure

Likely Question

Q: Explain systemic risk and operational risk in the context of e-security, with an example of each.

Systemic risk is the risk that an intrusion in one payment system's electronic messaging creates exposure across the entire interconnected system — e.g. a single hosting provider serving multiple banks becomes a single point of failure. Operational risk is the risk of service interruption or loss of critical information from inadequate e-security, including extortion and reputation risk that are not usually captured in standard operational-risk allocations.

Topic 4 Mrs O.O Akande

E-security in Emerging Markets

Five barriers that stop developing countries from implementing effective e-security.

Barriers to Implementing E-Security (as supplied)

  1. Rapid technological growth without proper regard to security — technologies like wireless adopted without understanding the risks; over-reliance on single "silver bullet" solutions (e.g. PKI) instead of a multi-layered approach.
  2. Lack of education on electronic risks for regulators, supervisors, system administrators and consumers — weakens trust and inhibits e-commerce.
  3. Lack of institutional infrastructure — legal, regulatory and law-enforcement capacity is weak or absent, and even existing regulation may not be enforced.
  4. Lack of social capital and technological "brain drain."
  5. High industry concentration in telecommunications — hosting/service provision often sits with one or a few entities, creating a single point of failure and conflicts of interest.
Plain EnglishDeveloping countries often adopt new technology faster than they can secure it, and don't have enough trained people, laws, or independent providers to fix the gap.
Topic 5 Mrs O.O Akande

The Public Interest and E-Security

Four arguments for why government has a legitimate role in regulating e-security.

  1. Critical infrastructure protection — financial services and payment systems are integral to a country's economic infrastructure; a breach can ripple into other infrastructures (e.g. telecoms hosting affecting banking).
  2. Classic market failure — financial providers have inadequate incentives to accurately report intrusions (reputation damage), so insurance markets can't price risk fairly; there is an asymmetric-information problem between technology buyers and vendors.
  3. Increasing returns to scale — IT markets tend toward concentration, requiring standardization/coordination, an effect magnified in smaller emerging markets (sometimes with government-owned telecom providers).
  4. Borderless, global reach of the Internet — mitigating risk requires unprecedented collective action within and between countries, which cannot be solved by private cooperation alone.
Plain EnglishBecause an e-security failure in one company can hurt the whole financial system — and because companies have incentives to hide breaches rather than report them — government has a real reason to step in and regulate.

The Electronic Security Industry and G-8 Principles

The growing e-security vendor industry, and the G-8's eleven principles for protecting critical information infrastructure.

The e-security industry spans active content filtering, intrusion detection, firewalls, penetration testing, encryption and authentication services. High cross-ownership between e-security and e-finance providers raises competition-policy concerns, and converging technologies (PC, landline, wireless, PDA) create new points of vulnerability — especially where a message "leaps" from one channel to another.

Topic 7 Mr O.I Olatunde

Tradeoffs: Security, Quality of Service, Privacy, Innovation, Costs

Four essential tradeoffs that any e-security policy must balance.

TradeoffWhat it means (as supplied)
a. Security and CostsSecurity should be proportional to transaction value — no case for expensive security (e.g. PKI) on small-value transactions when simpler authentication (tokens) suffices
b. Security and Quality of ServiceMore complex security (e.g. PKI) can slow transactions; but confidence in security is itself an aspect of service quality
c. Security and Technological InnovationRigid standards can stifle innovation — the concept of technology neutrality matters when drafting legislation (e.g. defining "electronic signature" carefully)
d. Security and PrivacyMore e-security can conflict with user privacy (e.g. tracking/verifying movement vs. anonymity in a trading strategy); the right balance is often culturally determined
Likely Question

Q: Explain the tradeoff between security and technological innovation.

Standards and guidelines are necessary for effective e-security, but rigid or non-neutral standards can stifle innovation if new technologies can't meet them. The course notes stress "technology neutrality" — for example, an electronic signature must be defined carefully enough not to preempt the use of alternative technologies — so legislation should remain flexible as technology evolves.

Topic 8 Mr O.I Olatunde

The Roles of the Private and Public Sectors in E-Security

What each sector is responsible for.

a. Roles of the Private Sector (as supplied)

  1. Secure its own electronic operations as ordinary business practice — internal monitoring is the first line of defence.
  2. Cooperate with academic institutions and governments to improve public education.
  3. Cooperate with law enforcement and supervisory authorities, including confidentiality guarantees when sharing breach information.
  4. Build internal governance/management structures (e.g. appoint a CISO — Chief Information Security Officer) and use industry/self-regulatory associations to set minimum standards.

b. Roles of the Public Sector (as supplied)

  1. Regulation — defining liability of parties/governance standards, enforceable legal concepts across borders, and standards/certification processes.
  2. Monitoring — supervision of e-finance providers, of private monitoring agents (insurers, credit-rating agencies), and of certification/technology providers.
  3. Promoting awareness and education — ongoing training and coordination between universities, government and the corporate sector worldwide.
Plain EnglishCompanies must secure themselves and be honest about breaches; government must set the rules, check that companies follow them, and help everyone understand the risks.
Topic 9 Mr O.I Olatunde

Security of Payment Systems and Privacy Law

Privacy law approaches, cyber crime jurisdiction, and the under-regulation of money transmitters and ISPs.

Two Approaches to Privacy Consent

ApproachMeaningUsed by
Opt-outConsent is assumed unless the person says noUnited States (financial activity)
Opt-inNo use of information is assumed unless the person consentsUnited States (medical information); European Union (all areas, per the 1990 EU Data Collection Directive)

Minimum privacy-law principles (Fair Information Practice Principles, adopted by the US Federal Trade Commission): notice, choice, access, and consent.

Cyber Crime & Jurisdiction

  • A December 2000 McConnell International survey of 52 countries found a "patchwork of outdated and inconsistent laws" effectively shielding cyber criminals from prosecution.
  • April 2002: unauthorized access to over 260,000 California state personnel files took six weeks to discover — California's response, Senate Bill 1386 (effective 1 July 2003), was the first US law mandating breach notification.
  • 2001: the Council of Europe's first international treaty on cyber crime addressed copyright infringement, computer-related fraud, child pornography and network security violations.
  • Ukraine and Belarus were cited as major staging grounds for organized hacker syndicates due to lack of cyber crime legislation.

Money Transmitters and ISPs

Money transmitters (money orders, wire transfers, currency exchange, check-cashing) and ISPs ("common carriers") are largely unregulated and not legally liable for the services they provide, even as they become more central to the payments system. Recommendation: legislation requiring them to be secure, post bond, and accept liability — possibly built on the Uniform Money Services Business Act.

Likely Question

Q: Compare the "opt-in" and "opt-out" approaches to privacy consent, and state which bodies use which.

Opt-out assumes consent to use personal information unless the individual affirmatively objects — the US uses this for financial activity. Opt-in assumes no consent unless the individual affirmatively gives it — the US uses this for medical information, and the EU uses it across the board, as set out in its 1990 Data Collection Directive. Both should, at minimum, embrace the Fair Information Practice Principles: notice, choice, access and consent.

Topic 10 Dr M.O Folami

Hand Tools and Powered Tools

Workplace tool-safety hazards, guards, and rules by tool power source.

Five Basic Safety Rules (as supplied)

  • Keep all tools in good condition with regular maintenance.
  • Use the right tool for the job.
  • Examine each tool for damage before use and do not use damaged tools.
  • Operate tools according to the manufacturer's instructions.
  • Provide and properly use the right personal protective equipment.

Guards & Operating Controls

Exposed moving parts (belts, gears, shafts, pulleys, sprockets, spindles, drums, flywheels, chains) must be guarded to protect against: point of operation, in-running nip points, rotating parts, and flying chips/sparks. Hand-held power tools above a size threshold (e.g. drills, grinders >2 in. diameter) must have a constant-pressure switch that cuts power when released; smaller tools may use a "lock-on" control instead.

Likely Question

Q: State the five basic safety rules for hand and power tools, and explain the main hazard associated with powder-actuated tools.

The five rules: keep tools in good condition, use the right tool for the job, inspect before use and discard damaged tools, follow manufacturer instructions, and use proper PPE. Powder-actuated tools "operate like a loaded gun" — they must be operated only by specially trained employees, require two separate motions to fire (reducing accidental discharge), and must never be pointed at anyone or left loaded and unattended.

Electronic Document Security

Encryption, digital signatures and PKI — the technical core of this course.

Why Document Security Matters (as supplied)

  1. Regulatory requirements — government mandates for consumer privacy.
  2. Return on investment (ROI) — automated workflows save cost, but can't be automated until the document itself is protected.
  3. Information security — theft of proprietary information damages revenue, competitive advantage and reputation.

Six criteria define persistent document security: Confidentiality, Authorization, Accountability, Integrity, Authenticity, Non-repudiation.

Document Control — 3 Pillars (as supplied)

PillarKey mechanisms
1. ConfidentialityEncryption (symmetric, asymmetric, hybrid)
2. AuthorizationPermissions (print/copy/sign/forward); dynamic document control (expiration/revocation, offline access limits, persistent version control)
3. AccountabilityDocument auditing — how, how often, and when a document was used, with automatic usage notifications

Digital Signatures: Integrity, Authenticity, Non-repudiation

RequirementHow it's achieved
IntegrityOne-way hash (MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256) or Message Authentication Code (MAC/HMAC) — detects alteration
AuthenticityAuthor's hash is encrypted with their private key; only their public key can correctly decrypt and verify it
Non-repudiationSupported by authentication and time-stamping (often via PKI) so the signer cannot deny signing

Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)

PKI provides a digital certificate binding a person/entity to a public key, issued by a Certificate Authority (CA) (standard: X.509). Validity is checked via a Certificate Revocation List (CRL) or the Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP). Other PKI components: PKCS (standard protocols), registration authority (background checks), certificate repository, key update/backup/recovery, cross-certification, and time stamping.

Likely Question

Q: Distinguish between symmetric and asymmetric key encryption, and explain why a hybrid approach is often used.

Symmetric key cryptography uses the same key for encryption and decryption — it is fast and strong with large keys, but key distribution is a problem since both parties need the same secret key (e.g. AES, DES, 3DES, RC4). Asymmetric (public key) cryptography uses a key pair — a public key anyone can use to encrypt, and a private key only the owner holds to decrypt — solving the distribution problem but running slower for equivalent strength (e.g. RSA, DSA). A hybrid approach uses the asymmetric keys to protect a symmetric key, then uses that fast symmetric key to encrypt the actual document — combining the security of asymmetric distribution with the speed of symmetric encryption.
📍 See Topic 11 → Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Encryption
Likely Question

Q: Describe the three steps involved in creating a digital signature, and explain what PKI adds to this process.

(1) A hash of the original document is created. (2) The digital signature is created by encrypting that hash with the signer's private key. (3) The signature is attached to the document. A recipient decrypts it with the signer's public key and compares it against a fresh hash of the received document — a match confirms both integrity and authenticity. PKI adds trust: a Certificate Authority issues a digital certificate (X.509 standard) binding the public key to a verified identity, and recipients can check the certificate's validity via a CRL or OCSP.

Electronic Security: Protecting Your Resources

Threat categories, the twelve layers of e-security, and ways to assess risk.

Three Main Areas of Electronic Threat (as supplied)

  1. Unauthorized External Access — targets financial details, intellectual property, trade secrets, confidential information.
  2. Unauthorized Internal Activities — trusted users disclosing valuable/confidential information, maliciously or unintentionally; often goes unnoticed since the user is acting within their assigned access level.
  3. Malware — viruses, worms, trojans, spyware, backdoors, rootkits; risk intensified by irresponsible or unaware staff.

Active vs. Passive Security

Plain EnglishActive security prevents a breach (e.g. installing a blocking application). Passive security monitors the system against the security model (e.g. reviewing server logs). The two strengthen each other.

Five Ways to Assess Risk (as supplied)

  • Quantitative risk assessment — threat frequency × loss amount vs. asset value (figures are often subjective)
  • Best practices — adopting accepted baselines (e.g. ISO17799, GASSP) to avoid a formal risk analysis
  • Scenario analysis — building mitigation plans for likely compromise scenarios
  • Cost-benefit analysis — weighing security cost against the value of the protected asset
  • Insuring all risks — insurance as the solution when a full security build-out isn't affordable

Likely Question

Q: Distinguish between the three main areas of electronic threat, with an example of each.

Unauthorized external access comes from outside the organization's network (e.g. a hacker targeting trade secrets over the Internet). Unauthorized internal activities come from trusted users who disclose confidential information, deliberately or by accident, often undetected because they are acting within their normal access rights. Malware is malicious software (viruses, worms, trojans, spyware, backdoors, rootkits) that infects systems connected to an external network, with risk worsened by careless or untrained staff.
📍 See Topic 12 → Three Main Areas of Electronic Threat
Likely Question

Q: Compare active and passive approaches to electronic security, and explain why both are needed.

An active approach covers actions designed to prevent a breach (e.g. deploying an application that blocks misuse once detected). A passive approach monitors the system against its security model (e.g. reviewing server logs with a tool like WebSpy). They strengthen each other: passive monitoring supplies the evidence (e.g. a user abusing resources) that justifies an active response (e.g. installing a restriction).
